How the fly works, in detail

What we simulated, what we changed, what we measured, and what did not work. Every number below comes from a script in the repository, listed at the end.

Rob Gourley, ClawStreet. September 2026.

The wiring

The network is the male CNS connectome, MaleCNS v1.0: a complete map of one male Drosophila melanogaster nervous system, traced from electron-microscope images by FlyEM at HHMI Janelia, Google Research and the Cambridge Connectomics Group. The paper reports 166,691 neurons. Our conversion keeps 164,587, the ones with a neurotransmitter prediction and at least one connection.

The neuron model is the one in Shiu et al. (Nature, 2024): leaky integrate-and-fire units with the same parameters for every cell. A connection's strength is its synapse count times one fixed weight. Acetylcholine excites; GABA and glutamate inhibit. Every synapse has the same 1.8 ms delay. We run it on Brian2.

That uniformity is the model's main limit, and it explains most of what failed further down. Real synapses differ in strength and timing. These do not.

Why only the smell circuit

Driving one smell channel in the whole-brain model lit 160 of the 4,064 Kenyon cells, the cells of the fly's learning centre. Driving a second, unrelated channel lit 157. The two sets shared 119 cells, 60%. Two smells that overlap that much cannot be told apart, so nothing can be learned about one without teaching the other.

The cause is the uniform weights. From a single channel, 2,215 Kenyon cells are reachable within two steps through regions that have nothing to do with smell. In the animal those routes are weak. Here a four-step detour arrives as loud as the direct path. Turning every synapse down does not help, because it weakens the direct path just as much:

So the flies run the smell circuit alone, 7,443 neurons: the receptor neurons, the relay neurons that take their input and pass it to Kenyon cells, the Kenyon cells, the APL neuron, the mushroom body output neurons, and the PAM and PPL1 dopamine neurons. Everything else is left out.

One change to the published model

The APL is a single large neuron that receives from all the Kenyon cells and inhibits all of them. In the real fly it does not fire spikes. It releases inhibition continuously, in proportion to how active the Kenyon cells are. As a spiking unit in the published model it fired at 363 Hz and suppressed almost nothing, so every smell lit a large, overlapping set of cells.

We replaced it with a graded version: Kenyon cell activity feeds a running level, and that level is subtracted from every Kenyon cell's drive. With the gain set to 250 on the smell circuit, 2 to 4% of Kenyon cells fire per smell. Recordings from real flies put it near 5%. The gain is ours, chosen to land in that range.

A second correction is in the converter for completeness, though the smell circuit does not use it: L1 neurons in the optic lobe are forced excitatory. L1 is glutamatergic, which the blanket rule makes inhibitory, but it excites its main target. Left alone it delivers 142,185 inhibitory synapses to Mi1 and silences the whole motion pathway.

From six indicators to a smell

Each symbol gives six readings from ClawStreet's market data: RSI, position in the Bollinger band, distance from the 50-day average, volume against its average, the five-day return, and whether RSI is rising, falling or flat. Each numeric reading is spread over eight overlapping bands, Gaussian, with a width of 0.75 band spacings, so a value activates its nearest band fully and its neighbours partly. The trend takes three on-off channels. News sentiment and days to earnings have channels too and are dark for now. That is 51 channels.

Each channel is assigned to one of the fly's glomeruli, the units of its smell map, in sorted order. The assignment is arbitrary. RSI has no natural glomerulus. What matters is that it is fixed, so the same setup always produces the same smell. A channel at full strength drives that glomerulus's receptor neurons with 100 Hz Poisson input. The circuit runs for 50 ms per symbol.

The verdict

Kenyon cells connect to 97 output neurons. We sort those by their own wiring, not by hand: an output neuron that gets more input from the PAM dopamine cluster is on the reward side, one that gets more from PPL1 is on the punishment side. That gives 41 and 56. For each Kenyon cell we add up its synapses onto each side. The verdict for a smell is the reward-side total minus the punishment-side total, over the cells that fired.

What it prefers untrained

Eight setups, four runs each. The spread between best and worst is 3.97 and the run-to-run noise is 0.19, so the ranking is 21 times the noise.

How far a lesson travels

One setup rewarded eight times, then the shift in verdict measured on setups at increasing distance. A setup sharing 35% of the trained cells received 57% of the shift. The opposite setup received 4%.

Learning, forgetting, selling

When a trade closes, the rule touches only the Kenyon cells that were firing when the fly chose it. After a profit, those cells' punishment-side weights are multiplied by 0.95. After a loss, their reward-side weights are. Both changes are depression, which is how the real synapse learns, so without anything else the weights would wear down to zero. Forgetting prevents that: every session each weight moves 2% of the way back to its starting value.

The sell rule compares today's verdict on a holding with the verdict at purchase. The same stock smelled on different days with unchanged readings gives verdicts up to 0.36 apart, from the Poisson input alone. A real change in the setup moves it by about 0.95. So a holding counts as liked less only when it drops by more than 0.36, and it is sold after two sessions in a row of that.

This does not behave like a stop loss. A position bought oversold and then recovering gets a higher verdict, because the untrained fly prefers breakouts. The rule fires when a holding goes quiet. Any drawdown limit would be a rule of ours, and there is none at present.

What the score measures

The numbers below do not measure trading skill. They measure whether the pattern of Kenyon cells that fire keeps the information that was in the six readings. We generated 200 synthetic setups and labelled each by a rule the fly is never shown: at least two of RSI under 45, volume above 1.3 times average, and Bollinger position under 0.4. A logistic regression then tries to recover the label from the firing pattern, scored by area under the ROC curve with five-fold cross-validation. Logistic regression is a stronger learner than the fly's own rule, so these are ceilings.

The fly's wiring scores 0.923 ± 0.049. A random sparse network with the same number of cells and the same sparseness scores 0.924 ± 0.024. The raw readings score 0.948 ± 0.044. The specific wiring adds nothing over a random one, and the expansion through Kenyon cells loses a little. That matches the standard view of the mushroom body as a random expansion, and it means nothing here is evidence the fly can pick stocks. Whether it can is what the live accounts are for.

What did not work

We tried several other ways to let the fly choose before settling on smell. Each failed for a reason in the model, and each is worth knowing before trying it again.

IdeaResult
Steer toward an odour by which side it is onOlfactory neurons send 51.9% of their output to each hemisphere, so the side is lost at the first synapse. Wind-sensing neurons keep 91.4% on their own side and do steer.
Tell a rising smell from a falling oneAt the same concentration the two differed by 0.5% in spikes. The units have no adaptation, so the network carries no history.
Detect motion in a moving chart6,793 motion detectors placed over eight chart regions produced two spikes between them. Direction selectivity comes from differences in synaptic delay, and every delay here is 1.8 ms.
Read the shape of a chart by sightObject-detecting neurons responded to a rising and a falling chart with a correlation of 0.990. The response tracks how much is drawn, not what.
